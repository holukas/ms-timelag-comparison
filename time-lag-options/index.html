Choosing a time-lag option

ETH Zürich

Five time-lag options are compared in this study (see processing versions for the exact settings). Four of them start from one exploratory run, CM-W, and use its peak lag distribution to configure themselves; the fifth, PWB OPT, is an independent method. The flowchart shows how they relate, and the notes below say when each is appropriate.

The five variants compared in this study. CM-W (variant 1) is one wide covariance-maximisation run (Nemitz steps 1 to 3): it keeps its own lags and yields the distribution peak (nominal lag) and the distribution range. The peak is the default fallback for variants 2 and 3 and the fixed lag for variant 4; the range sets the narrow window for variant 3. PWB OPT (variant 5) is independent. The Nemitz et al. When to use what¶

1. CM-W (wide window, no default). The exploratory first pass. It searches the whole 0 to 10 s window every half-hour with no fallback, so its output reveals the lag distribution and its modal peak, which options 2 to 4 build on. With nothing constraining the search, it is the most exposed to the covariance-maximisation (“mirroring”) bias for low signal-to-noise gases like N₂O: per half-hour the lag that maximises the absolute covariance is picked, inflating the flux away from zero. Covariance can be maximised against the scalar S of interest itself, or against an inert gas on the same analyzer (shared sampling line, so the lag is easier to detect), whose lag is then applied to S. Use it to characterise the lag and configure 2 to 4; treat its flux as the upper bound of that bias, not the final answer.

2. CM-W + default. The same wide window, but it falls back to the nominal lag (the CM-W peak) whenever the search is unreliable. Use it for automatic per-half-hour lags with a safety net against obviously wrong picks. It still carries most of the mirroring bias, since the search range is unchanged.

3. CM-W CTR + default. The search is constrained to a narrow window around the peak, with the same fallback. The narrow window curbs the mirroring bias while still letting the lag track real variation within a known range. Use it when the true lag is well constrained and reasonably stable. This is the recommended covariance-maximisation option.

4. Constant lag. A single fixed lag at the CM-W peak, applied to every half-hour. Use it when the lag is physically stable (for example a steady closed-path setup) and you want to remove the covariance-maximisation bias entirely. Most conservative budget, but ignores any real lag variation over time.

5. PWB OPT. Independent of covariance maximisation and robust to the mirroring bias. It is the reference method (Vitale et al. 2024): the benchmark the covariance-maximisation options are judged against, and the default when a bias-resistant lag is wanted without tuning a window by hand.

Rule of thumb¶

  • Run CM-W first: its peak distribution sets up 2 to 4, and its flux shows how large the covariance-maximisation bias is for your gas.

  • For a covariance-maximisation flux, prefer the constrained CM-W CTR over the wide-window variants: the narrow window is what limits the bias.

  • Use the constant lag only where the lag is genuinely stable.

  • Reach for PWB OPT when you want a bias-resistant lag with no window to tune, or as the reference the other options are compared against.
